1.2.1·Electronegativity and the Truth of Bonding

Definition and Explanation

Atoms rely on the attractive force of its positive nucleus on surrounding electrons to keep their structure, and so when atoms form a chemical bond, the electrons involved in that bond are now being shared by the pull of the different nuclei. This begs the question, if these atoms are pulling electrons towards themselves, which atom gets to pull the hardest?

The answer depends on a property called electronegativity: an atom's ability to attract shared electrons toward itself in a chemical bond.

Electronegativity is mainly affected by two factors: the number of protons, and the placement of its electrons.

The reasoning of protons is relatively obvious to visualize. The more protons an atom has, the more positively charged its nucleus is. The more positively charged its nucleus, the more attractive it is to negatively charged electrons. In general, a nucleus with more protons has stronger attractive pull.

The placement of electrons is harder to visualize, but recall that electrons are arranged in different energy levels and shells. Electrons closer to the nucleus would sit between the nucleus and electrons further away, right?

Because of their like negative charge, electrons will repel one another. This means that inner electrons partially reduce the attractive force of the nucleus on the outer electrons. This is called shielding, and its effect means that the outer electrons will not feel the full positive charge from the nucleus.
