1.2.2·Electronegativity and the Truth of Bonding

Electronegativity in Bonding

You’ve most likely had to deal with ionic and covalent bonds before. As a quick review, ionic bonds happen when an electron is transferred from one atom to another, while covalent bonds happen when an electron pair is “shared’ between two atoms. Seems simple enough, but where exactly is the line between a covalent bond and an ionic bond?

The answer is electronegativity! Let’s compare three different bonding situations, each with a different difference of electronegativity between two atoms (note that the diagram shown is extremely simplified, in reality, electrons are shared in pairs, and would be constantly moving. The diagrams show where the electron pairs would be on average, and not strictly.):

  1. Both atoms share the same electronegative value.

If both atoms share similar or the exact electronegative value, then the electron pair is shared perfectly between the two atoms. This would be considered the purest form of a covalent bond, where the electron pair would be found, on average, in the middle of the two atoms.

  1. One atom is slightly more electronegative than the other.

If one atom is slightly more electronegative than the other, the electron pair would be found closer towards the more electronegative atom. This bond can still be considered “covalent” depending on the difference in electronegativity, as the electron pair is still somewhat being attracted by the less electronegative atom.

Do note that because the electron pair is slightly more towards one side, that specific atom is now slightly negative, while the other atom is now slightly positive. Scientists often use the Greek letter δ (delta) to denote “slightly,” and so you may often see the more negative atom denoted as “δ-,” and the more positive atom denoted as “δ+,” meaning “slightly negative” and “slightly positive” respectively.

These bonds are also often called “polar bonds,” polar, as in two ends, one negative, and one positive. Polar bonds are special covalent bonds in which there is a slight separation in charge between the two atoms, making one side more negative than the other, and vice versa.

Yes, these bonds formed at slightly different electronegative values are pretty complex, so don’t worry, we’ll be looking at them again in future lessons.

  1. One atom is a lot more electronegative than the other.

In this case, the electron pair has been almost completely taken by the more electronegative atom, forming ions. The less electronegative atom has lost most of its control over its electrons, turning it into a cation with a positive charge. The more electronegative atom now has near control over both electrons, turning it into an anion with a negative charge.

This is different from the “slightly negative/positive” charges shown in the second case. Because the electron pair is now almost entirely controlled by the more electronegative atom, its charge can be described as being -1, as in, negative one charge. This also works for the less electronegative atom, whose charge can be described as +1, as in, positive one charge.

Please note that usually, bonds form in long chains and lattices, meaning that the interactions between atoms actually extends numerously in all directions. Because of this, electron pairs usually feel the force of attraction of billions of atoms, in a million directions, unlike the simplified diagrams shown here.

You may have noticed that there is not a single clearly defined line between what bonds count as ionic or covalent. That’s because there is none! Although scientists have classified a wide range of covalent and ionic bonds, the exact cut-off point between what counts is impossible to know.

Let’s look back at the definition of a polar bond, that being a special covalent bond in which there is a separation of charge. A more accurate picture of bonding is that it is a spectrum, from nonpolar, to covalent polar, to highly polar, to ionic. The terms “covalent” and “ionic” simply represent the two extreme ends of this spectrum. In fact, almost all bonds are not entirely covalent or ionic, and sit inside this spectrum, sharing both ionic and covalent properties depending on their electronegative differences.

A good example of this is Hydrogen Chloride (HCl), a covalent compound by definition, but due to chlorine being more electronegative than hydrogen, forms a highly polar bond. In fact, when dissolved in water, hydrogen chloride fully ionizes into separate negative chlorine ions and positive hydrogen ions.
