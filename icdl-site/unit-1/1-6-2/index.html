1.6.2·Properties of Elements, Relooked

Atomic and Ionic Radii

How big is an atom? Obviously we can’t just take a ruler and start measuring a quantum object, so how can we define the actual size of atoms?

Recall that in the quantum mechanical model, an orbital is defined as a region in the atom where there is a high probability of an electron being found. Because we can never find a sharp boundary for the size of orbitals, we also cannot define a perfect, definite size of atoms.

However, recall that some elements can bond with themselves, forming what are called “homonuclear” bonds. Some common examples include nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and more.

With these bonds, we can measure the distance between the identical nuclei with relatively high accuracy, and then divide the answer by two to get the “covalent radius” of the atom. For heteronuclear bonds, that is, bonds with different elements, you must subtract the value of a known covalent radius from the total distance between the nuclei.

In a metallic crystal lattice, atoms are slightly further apart from each other than compared to a covalent bond, as there is less overlap between overalls, and more a large lattice of metallic ions in a sea of valence electrons. The general idea is still the same, measuring the distance between two nuclei and dividing by two, but the size is usually smaller than covalent radii. Because of this, scientists call this specific type of measured radius a “metallic radius.”

What happens if two atoms cannot bond together? If you measure the distance between two adjacent, non-bonded atoms of the same element at their closest distance and divide it by two, you find the Van der Waals radius, named after the weak intermolecular forces that keep these atoms close. We will discuss Van der Waals forces and other intermolecular forces in a future lesson.

Awesome, but these numbers do not represent the definite size of an atom. The main issue with covalent and Van der Waals radii is that an atom’s size is not constant, it is based on its specific chemical environment. Any slight change in its surroundings can drastically change its size.

So why even bother measuring if they aren’t even accurate? Because with these measured radii, we can form a basic pattern and list of elements.
