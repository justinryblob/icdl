It is common for school teachers to explain chemical reactions and bonding by saying that atoms of different elements attempt to achieve the electron configuration of a noble gas. While this is a useful generalization, the deeper truth is that atoms do not bond simply because they are trying to “be like” noble gases. In fact, there is nothing uniquely desirable about being a noble gas. The real explanation is much more fundamental and mathematical. Atoms, like many physical systems, tend to evolve toward states that are more energetically favourable under the conditions they are in.

Of course, atoms are not sentient and do not actually have desires, but we can think of them as “lazy” as in they naturally tend toward arrangements that are more favourable and require less free energy to maintain. This metaphor can help us understand why atoms behave the way they do and why they form bonds and compounds, instead of just staying inert.

At every moment, atoms are subject to a variety of interactions. Electrons repel one another, electrons are attracted to nuclei, and nuclei repel one another. These interactions create a sort of energy landscape in which some arrangements of particles are more favourable than others. A chemical bond forms when bringing atoms together can produce a more favourable overall state than keeping them separate.

This is the deeper picture behind chemical bonding, perhaps a little too complex for a first lesson, but good to remember. Instead of thinking that atoms are consciously “wanting” a particular electron configuration, their behaviour emerges from the fundamental interactions between charged particles and the physical laws governing energy and matter.

For now though, just remember that when a system can move toward a state of lower potential energy, that change can be energetically favourable.

Remember this! This idea will become especially important later, when we introduce thermodynamics and entropy.
