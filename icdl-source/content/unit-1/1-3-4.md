So the quantum mechanical model is perfect and explains the atom flawlessly, right?

Well… not really.

The quantum mechanical model is the best and most present model of the atom we have so far, but there are still some properties of the atom it overlooks or cannot explain, as well as multiple issues and limitations within the model itself.

This section will briefly go over some of the major problems with the quantum mechanical model, not for any major reasons, but to emphasize that chemistry is still young, and that the atom is still far from solved. Note that I will not mention all issues with the model, just the most obvious and relevant for now.

### 1. Not Visually Intuitive

You’ve probably already experienced this. How can you intuitively visualize atoms and electrons as waves of probability? Moreover, how can you visualize these waves of probability interacting with each other to form bonds? The truth is you can’t, I can’t, most people can’t. For all intents and purposes, especially at this level, it is still easier to just use the Bohr model for most calculations and situations dealing with atoms.

### 2. Theoretically Incomplete

This is more theoretical and higher concept-wise, but you may recall that one of, if not the biggest unsolved problem in all of physics is connecting the physics of the very small (quantum mechanics) to the physics of the very large (general relativity). Although both extremes have figured out ways to predict and explain their own worlds pretty well, trying to connect these two poles of physics together has proven to be a very difficult task.

### 3. The Many-Body Problem

You may have seen or even understood Schrödinger’s equation before, and know how it is meant to describe the quantum state of a system as it changes over time. However, actual analytical solutions to Schrödinger’s equation only exist for single-electron systems, like hydrogen. Because the wave function scales exponentially with the number of particles, even double-electron systems like helium are completely infeasible to calculate with our current technology.

By understanding the quantum mechanical model, I hope you can actually gain a newfound appreciation for the earlier models. They are not “wrong” in the sense, but tailored to a more general and simpler use for atoms.
